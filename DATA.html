💡 Idiot's guide to the company — What GlobalData Plc actually does

What it is

GlobalData Plc (LSE: DATA, previously AIM) is a subscription data and analytics business headquartered in London. It sells proprietary intelligence — industry research, competitive data, clinical trials data, market forecasts — to large corporates across healthcare, technology, financial services, consumer, and other sectors. Think of it as a vast private library of structured data that clients access via annual subscription, rather than hiring their own research teams.

Revenue streams

Roughly 75% subscription (recognised evenly over the contract term); 25% project-based consulting and events. About half of revenue is billed in US dollars. Healthcare (formerly partially sold to Inflexion PE at 40% in 2024) and Non-Healthcare (Technology, Consumer, Professional Services, Industrials) are the two reporting segments — Healthcare contributes ~38% of Group revenue at higher margins (~50%).

How it grows

Organic growth comes from four levers: renewing existing clients at higher value (pricing), adding more licences within existing clients (seats), selling additional products (cross-sell), and winning new logos. M&A adds both content and new client bases — the company acquired 6 businesses in FY24–25 alone, more than doubling the deal pace of prior years.

Recent structural events

June 2024: Inflexion Private Equity took a 40% stake in the Healthcare division for £451m gross, reshaping the balance sheet from £244m net debt to near-zero. Dec 2024 — Jul 2025: four further acquisitions (BTMI, LinkUp, Celent, Deallus, Ai Palette, Stylus). March 2026: moved from AIM to LSE Main Market. CFO Graham Lilley departing; replacement Rob Kingston (ex-Keywords Studios, ex-Sky) joining Q3 2026.

The investment argument: a defensive recurring-revenue data platform with 80%+ revenue visibility entering each year, capable of compounding EBITDA margins toward 40%+ as the sales transformation embeds — but the organic growth engine has to actually accelerate first. As at Jul-26.

🍺 IDIOT'S GUIDE TO THE ADJ. EBITDA BRIDGE — why profit went down (£116.8m → £110.2m)
💼 Six new companies moved in and each one needed a complete renovation
−c.£15m
Four acquisitions landed Q4 FY24 (LinkUp, Celent, Deallus, BTMI) then Ai Palette and Stylus in FY25. None was cheap to run before it had been bolted into the platform, and none had yet delivered a pound of revenue synergy — management itself says those synergies "remain a significant opportunity," which is the polite version of "not yet."
🏋️ Hired 90 extra salespeople and rearranged the whole shop floor
−c.£10m
Sales headcount went from 277 (Jan 2024) to 366 (Jan 2025): two new Chief Revenue Officers, a full strategic-account-management layer, a revenue operations team. The headcount grew faster than the revenue it was meant to generate. Standard year-one transformation tax.
💱 The pound got stronger; the dollar got smaller
−c.£4m
About half of Group revenue is billed in USD. When sterling strengthens, those revenues shrink in the annual report even though clients paid the same in dollars. Nothing operationally wrong — purely a translation effect.
📈 The sticky subscription engine kept ticking over
+c.£3m
Consistent renewal rates and modest price increases meant the core subscription book crept forward. It's just that the three items above each individually outweighed the organic progress.
So in one sentence: The company brought in £39m of extra revenue via acquisitions, but actually spent £46m buying and running those acquisitions plus rebuilding its sales machine — so adjusted profit fell £7m despite the top line looking like a growth year.

Profit goes back up when: the acquired businesses stop burning cash on arrival and start generating their planned £8m-plus of synergies (management says this was "largely complete by Dec-25"); the 366-person sales team starts landing the seven-figure enterprise contracts it was hired for (three were signed in H2 FY25 — encouraging but early); and the pound stops eating the dollar revenues. None of that requires anything heroic — it needs time, execution, and sales cycles that don't keep lengthening on them.
