💡Idiot’s guide to the company — What Luceco plc actually does

What they sell

The electrical bits inside a building. Plug sockets, light switches, LED light fittings, cable trunking, extension leads — and, increasingly, chargers for electric cars. If it is screwed to a wall and something plugs into it, Luceco probably makes a version of it. Head office is in Telford, Shropshire; most of it is made in the group’s own factory in Jiaxing, China. Brands include BG, Masterplug, DW Windsor, D-Line and CMD.

Where the money comes from

Two engines. The first is the everyday kit, sold three ways: to trade wholesalers, straight into building projects, and onto the shelves of DIY retailers. Steady, unexciting, and the bulk of sales.

The second is EV charging, and it earns twice. Once when a charger is sold. Then again, month after month, through something called Demand Flexibility: when the national electricity network is straining at peak times, an enrolled charger eases off, and Luceco is paid for that. More than 18,000 chargers were signed up as at May 2026.

Who sets the prices

Luceco sets its own on the everyday kit, and has been pushing prices up to cover pricier raw materials like copper. It does not set the price on Demand Flexibility — the electricity regulator writes those rules, and it rewrote them in mid-2026 so each charger earns less than it did. That is the single biggest thing outside management’s hands.

What’s changed lately

Two purchases bolted on: D-Line in February 2024 (£7.8m) and CMD in September 2024 (£29.7m), both cable and wiring businesses. Demand Flexibility income only started in 2025. And John Hornby, who ran the place for 21 years, retires on 31 December 2026 — no successor named yet.

The one-line version: a dull, cash-generative maker of sockets and switches that has accidentally grown a fast, regulated, high-margin electricity business on the side — and the argument is about how much that second bit is really worth.

Turn methodology & restatement basis

Comparison bases. Every Δ and arrow is computed in one script with the basis hard-coded per metric type: snapshot metrics (the FY guide) compare position-to-position at their own disclosure grain; flow metrics (Energy Transition revenue, quarterly revenue) compare like-period-to-like-period only (H1-vs-H1, never H1-vs-9M); rate metrics (LFL/organic %, margin) are seasonally matched — H1-vs-H1, FY-vs-FY, Q-vs-same-Q. Sequential QonQ fires 🔴↘ on any negative quarter regardless of year-comparison (primary, Q-grain); the rate trigger is banded H-grain ±2.0pp / Q-grain ±5.0pp (secondary). Guides never fire the sequential/rate triggers — those are actuals-only.

🍺 IDIOT’S GUIDE TO THE ADJUSTED OPERATING PROFIT BRIDGE — why profit went up (£33.8m → £40m+)
🔌 The plug that pays you back
+c.120%
Luceco’s car chargers now earn twice. Once when someone buys the box. Then again, quietly, every time the charger eases off while the national grid is straining — money that barely existed two years ago.
🧱 Sockets, switches, boring, brilliant
+c.6%
The bread-and-butter kit — wall sockets, light fittings, cable trunking — kept growing at a steady clip. Nobody photographs a socket, and it still pays for most of the building.
⚡ The regulator trims the tap
n/q
From early in the second half, the rules deciding what a charger earns for easing off get tightened, so each one brings in less per year. Management says it saw this coming, and the £40m still stands.
💰 Copper, passed down the wire
+0.1pp
Raw material prices kept climbing. Rather than swallow it, Luceco nudged its own prices up — so profit per £1 of sales still edged ahead, 11.0% to 11.1%.
So in one sentence: car-charger income roughly doubled and the everyday kit kept selling, which is what lifts profit from £33.8m towards £40m — and the regulator takes a slice of the charger money back from the summer onwards.
Profit falls when: the trimmed charger payments turn out smaller than the Board expects; core sales slip below their c.6% pace; or copper climbs faster than customers will accept a price rise.
