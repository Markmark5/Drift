💡Idiot's guide to the company — What Billington Holdings actually does

The one-liner

Billington makes the steel skeletons of big buildings. Somebody wins a job to build a data centre, a power-from-rubbish plant or a warehouse; Billington designs, cuts, welds and bolts together the steel frame that holds it all up, then erects it on site.

Where it sits

A small Yorkshire outfit, listed on London's junior AIM market, run out of Barnsley with a heritage going back 75 years. Two big fabrication sheds in Barnsley (Wombwell and Shafton) do the heavy steel; a Bristol site called Yate was shut at the end of 2025 and its work moved north.

How it actually earns

Two halves. The big one, Structural Steelwork (about 87% of sales), is the frames — plus a stairs business (Peter Marshall), a bridge specialist (Tubecon), and a painting/coatings arm (SPC). The small one, Safety Solutions, hires out temporary edge-protection barriers (Easi-Edge) and site hoarding with graphics (Hoard-it). The trick of the whole thing: revenue tracks how much steel goes into a job, but the profit tracks how many hours the welders put in. Fiddly, complex jobs use few tonnes and lots of hours — so sales can fall while the factory is busier than ever.

Who holds the reins

Family-influenced and tightly held — only about 13.3m shares exist. Mark Smith is CEO; Trevor Taylor moved from finance to Chief Operating Officer in October 2025 and Dave Jones stepped up to finance chief. Debt-free, sitting on roughly £20m of cash, which is most of the investment case.

The recent big move

Closing Yate and pulling everything into Barnsley — a one-off £2.8m bill in 2025 meant to lower the running cost per hour from 2026 onward.

The argument, in one line: a debt-free, well-run niche fabricator riding out a soft construction market on its cash pile, betting that delayed jobs and a leaner cost base turn a bad 2025 into a much better 2026.

🍺 IDIOT'S GUIDE TO THE PROFIT BEFORE TAX BRIDGE — why profit went down (£10.8m → £1.3m)
🔩 Same sweat, smaller invoice
n/q
They worked 4.2% more hours than the year before and billed 15.4% less. The jobs got fiddlier — power stations and bridges instead of big plain sheds — and a fiddly job eats a fortnight of welding but hardly any steel. You bill for the steel.
🩸 Neighbours bidding at daft money
n/q
Rival fabricators with empty factories priced work at whatever kept the lights on. Billington refused to chase them down, which protected its name and cost it the volume.
⏸️ Clients pressed pause
−£6.7m
Customers kept enlarging jobs and then delaying them. Profit isn't booked until the steel goes up, so the money didn't vanish — it slid sideways into 2026. Three of these together took £6.7m off the profit line.
🚪 Waving off Bristol
−£2.8m
Shutting the Yate factory and moving the work to Barnsley. Redundancies, write-downs, machinery on lorries up the M5. A one-off bill, paid in the back half of the year.
So in one sentence: they put in more hours than ever, got paid less for every one of them, and then paid £2.8m to close a factory.
Profit goes back up when: the delayed jobs actually get built in 2026 and the deferred margin lands; steel per hour rises again as plainer contracts return to the mix; and Barnsley's lower cost per hour shows up in the Structural Steelwork margin — it needs to travel from 2.3% back toward the high single digits, and the last half we can see went the wrong way on price.
