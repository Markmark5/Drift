💡Idiot's guide to the company — What Renishaw plc actually does

What it is

Renishaw makes the measuring kit that other factories rely on to check their own work. If a jet-engine blade has to be right to a fraction of a hair's width, somebody is checking it with a Renishaw probe. It's an engineering business, listed in the UK, and it has been at this for fifty years.

Where it's based

Head office and most of the manufacturing sit at Wotton-under-Edge in Gloucestershire, with a second big UK site in South Wales. Most of the money comes from abroad — Asia, the US and Europe — which is why the exchange rate matters so much to the profit line.

How it makes money

Three streams. Industrial Metrology is the big one: probes and software that sit inside machine tools and measuring machines, sold to anyone cutting metal. Position Measurement sells encoders — the parts that tell a machine exactly where its own moving bits are — and these go heavily into semiconductor and electronics equipment. Specialised Technologies is the smaller pot: 3D printing, medical and scientific instruments.

Who controls the price. Renishaw largely does. It sells patented precision parts into machines that would be worthless without them, so it isn't discounting to win work. What it can't control is volume — when chip makers or car plants stop buying new machines, the orders simply stop coming, and that's what drives the good years and the bad ones.

What's changed recently

The company cut roughly £20m a year off the wage bill from the first half of FY26, renamed its reporting segments in October 2025, and changed finance director — Allen Roberts left at the November 2025 AGM and John Shipsey was confirmed in the job by April 2026.

The bet, in one line: you are buying a toll-booth on precision manufacturing, and paying for the patience to sit through the order cycle.

🍺 IDIOT'S GUIDE TO THE ADJUSTED PBT BRIDGE — why profit went up (£127.2m → ~£167m)
📦 They just shipped more kit
n/q
More CMMs, more encoders, more lasers went out the factory door in Wotton-under-Edge this year than last — about £100m more revenue, and most of it fell straight to the bottom line, because the factory was already built and the lights were already on. This is the whole story, really; everything else below is just noise around the edges. RNS doesn't give a clean profit number for this bit, so we can't print one — just know it's carrying the team.
💷 The pound played dirty
−£13.2m
Picture a chip fab in Taiwan paying Renishaw in dollars, and by the time that cash lands in Gloucestershire, the exchange rate has quietly pickpocketed a chunk of it. £5.2m lost purely because sterling strengthened, plus another £8.0m because a batch of insurance-style currency bets that paid out nicely LAST year simply expired this year and weren't replaced by anything as good. Nobody did anything wrong here — the money just got smaller crossing the Atlantic (H1 number; nobody's said what the full year looks like).
🛃 Trump's tariffs, shrugged off
~£0m
Uncle Sam slapped roughly £5m of import tax on kit heading into the States, so Renishaw just quietly bumped the price tag by the same amount and let American customers foot it. Profit: untouched. The only casualty is the margin PERCENTAGE — extra revenue with zero extra profit attached pulls the ratio down a hair even though the pound-notes number doesn't move.
✂️ The £20m haircut
n/q
Back in June the company took the razor to its wage bill — roughly £20m a year of payroll gone, mostly people, not products — and that saving is now baked in every month rather than a one-off. It's the reason the margin is clawing its way back toward the 20% finish line the company's been chasing for years, even with the pound working against them. Don't get too excited though — you only get to cut a cost once; next year this line has nothing left to give unless they find a new set of scissors.
So in one sentence: they sold a lot more, the currency gods took a bite, Washington's tariffs were a non-event, and last year's cost-cutting is still paying rent. Profit goes back up when: sterling stops being so strong against the dollar (that £13.2m stops disappearing), or the order book keeps converting into shipped machines at this pace without needing a second round of job cuts to prop up the margin.
