💡Idiot's guide to the company — What Renishaw plc actually does

What it is

Renishaw makes the measuring kit that other factories rely on to check their own work. If a jet-engine blade has to be right to a fraction of a hair's width, somebody is checking it with a Renishaw probe. It's an engineering business, listed in the UK, and it has been at this for fifty years.

Where it's based

Head office and most of the manufacturing sit at Wotton-under-Edge in Gloucestershire, with a second big UK site in South Wales. Most of the money comes from abroad — Asia, the US and Europe — which is why the exchange rate matters so much to the profit line.

How it makes money

Three streams. Industrial Metrology is the big one: probes and software that sit inside machine tools and measuring machines, sold to anyone cutting metal. Position Measurement sells encoders — the parts that tell a machine exactly where its own moving bits are — and these go heavily into semiconductor and electronics equipment. Specialised Technologies is the smaller pot: 3D printing, medical and scientific instruments.

Who controls the price. Renishaw largely does. It sells patented precision parts into machines that would be worthless without them, so it isn't discounting to win work. What it can't control is volume — when chip makers or car plants stop buying new machines, the orders simply stop coming, and that's what drives the good years and the bad ones.

What's changed recently

The company cut roughly £20m a year off the wage bill from the first half of FY26, renamed its reporting segments in October 2025, and changed finance director — Allen Roberts left at the November 2025 AGM and John Shipsey was confirmed in the job by April 2026, presenting his first full-year results in September. New segment directors now run each of the three businesses.

The bet, in one line: you are buying a toll-booth on precision manufacturing, and paying for the patience to sit through the order cycle.

🍺 IDIOT'S GUIDE TO THE ADJUSTED PBT BRIDGE — why profit went up (£127.2m → £168.0m)⭐ FY R · 23 Sep 2026
🏭 Encoder lines on double time
+4.4pt margin
Chip-equipment builders couldn't get enough of Renishaw's position sensors, so sales grew far faster than the cost of the engineers, sales teams and offices behind them. Same building, same payroll, a lot more boxes going out the door. That's worth 4.4 points of margin on its own — and it's after paying out £6.9m more in staff bonuses for the privilege.
✂️ Last year's job cuts paid out
+£20m
The cost programme started last year finally landed in full: about £20m saved, 2.5 points of margin. Here's the catch nobody puts in the headline — making those cuts cost £18.0m in redundancy and one-offs, which the company leaves out of the "adjusted" profit. So you paid for the diet and the gym membership in the same year.
💱 The mini-Budget bonus ran dry
−2.2pt margin
Back in autumn 2022, when sterling crashed after the mini-Budget, Renishaw locked in lovely exchange rates for its foreign sales. Those deals padded H1 FY25 handsomely and have now expired. Converting dollars and yen back into pounds simply pays less than it did — 2.2 points of margin gone, and no one did anything wrong.
🗑️ A sensor they had to shelve
−£8.8m
£5.3m written off on a new encoder the company pulled its people away from — because the OLD encoders were selling too fast to staff both. Rarely has a write-off been such a humblebrag. The other £3.5m is property in Slovenia and the UK marked down in value.
So in one sentence: busy chip customers filled the factory, the job cuts showed up in full, and that easily outran a currency deal expiring and one product put on the shelf. Profit falls when: semiconductor makers stop ordering new kit and those same encoder lines go quiet — the extra margin from being busy vanishes just as fast as it arrived — and there isn't a second £20m of cuts left to fall back on.
