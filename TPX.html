💡Idiot's guide to the company — What TPXimpact actually does

What it is

TPXimpact builds and fixes the digital services the UK government runs on — the website you renew a licence on, the case system a court clerk uses, the data platform behind a farm payment. Clients are departments: Ministry of Justice, DEFRA, NHS England, HM Land Registry.

Where it's based

UK, listed on AIM. Around 700 people including associates at the March 2026 year-end.

How it makes money

Almost all of it is people's time sold by the day, on contracts won through government tender frameworks such as Digital Outcomes and Specialists. Three parts: digital transformation work for government (the large majority), a smaller agency arm called manifesto, and a technology services unit.

Who controls the price. The government does. Frameworks cap day rates and departments decide when work is awarded and how fast it starts — when the Treasury tightens, awards stop, which is what happened in 2024.

What's changed recently

A three-year repair job ran from 2023 to 2026 and finished on target: two overseas units sold in 2023, debt cleared by September 2026, profitability multiplied several times over. A new three-year growth plan started in FY27, with a Chief Growth Officer appointed to run it.

The bet, in one line: a repaired business whose new contracts are now billing — the open question is whether government keeps signing at this pace.

🍺 IDIOT'S GUIDE TO THE ADJUSTED EBITDA BRIDGE — why profit went up (£3.2m → £8.0m)⭐ H1 R · 10 Nov 2026

 
🚂 Whitehall's new wagons hitched
+£6.1m
The justice, prisons and Land Registry contracts signed last year are now billing flat out — £21.7m more revenue became £6.1m more gross profit, people charging by the day.

 
🏋️ Head office didn't bulk up
−£1.3m
Running costs rose only £1.3m while sales jumped 60% — the same back office now carries a far heavier load, and the spare drops straight through to profit.

 
🧑‍🔧 Hired guns cost a sliver
n/q
To staff the new wins fast they used more freelance contractors, who cost more than employees — each £1 of sales kept 30p instead of 31p. A small toll for mobilising at this speed.

 
So in one sentence: last year's contract wins all started paying at once, head office stayed lean, and profit went from £3.2m to £8.0m.

 Profit falls when: the big contracts pause between phases, or hiring permanent staff to replace the pricier freelancers lags the next wave of work.
