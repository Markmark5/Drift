💡 Idiot's guide to the company — What 4imprint Group plc actually does
What it sells

4imprint puts a company's name on things people keep. Pens, water bottles, tote bags, hoodies, notebooks, lanyards — a school orders 300 branded T-shirts for a fun run, a plumbing firm orders 2,000 pens with its phone number on them. Roughly 2 million such orders a year, average around $650 each.

How the money actually works

It never touches the goods. A customer orders through the website or catalogue, 4imprint sends the order and the artwork to one of its supplier factories, and the factory prints the item and ships it straight to the customer. 4imprint keeps the difference between what the customer pays and what the factory charges — about 32 cents in every dollar. Because there is almost no stock and most customers pay by card, money comes in before it goes out, which is why the company runs with cash in the bank and no borrowings at all.

Finding customers is the whole game, and it costs money: about 13 cents of every dollar of sales goes on advertising, catalogues and search. The company watches one number above all others — how many dollars of sales each advertising dollar brings back. It has been running near $7.80.

Where it is and who runs it

Listed in London, but the business is almost entirely American: 98% of sales come from North America, run out of Oshkosh, Wisconsin, with a small operation in the UK and Ireland. Accounts are in US dollars, dividends paid in sterling. Kevin Lyons-Tarr has been chief executive throughout. The finance chief changed in 2025 and the chairman changed in 2026, so two of the three people signing the accounts are new.

What is happening right now

US import tariffs have pushed up what the supplier factories charge. 4imprint has raised its own prices a little to cover part of it, but not all, so the slice it keeps on each order has shrunk for the first time in four years. Separately, fewer brand-new customers have been signing up since 2023 — existing ones keep reordering perfectly happily, but the top of the funnel narrowed. That decline started easing in the first half of 2026.

The argument in one line: a debt-free, cash-rich business with a genuinely durable customer base is taking a squeeze on both the number of new customers and the margin on each order, and the question is whether the easing in new customers arrives before the tariff squeeze does more damage.

🍺 IDIOT'S GUIDE TO THE PROFIT BRIDGE — why profit went down ($74.0m → $64.8m)
📦 The tariff bill stapled to every box
−$8.9m
The factories that print the mugs and hoodies put their prices up because of US import taxes. 4imprint kept 31.5 cents of each sales dollar instead of 32.8 cents. On this much business, that missing 1.3 cents is the whole story.
🖊️ Pricier pens, fewer of them
+$2.3m
It nudged its own prices up — the typical order got 3% dearer — while 1% fewer orders came in. Net of the two, a bit more money through the door. It clawed back roughly a quarter of the tariff bill this way.
📡 Feeding the advertising machine
−$1.6m
Adverts, catalogues, search listings and the people answering the phones all cost a shade more than last year. Nothing has gone wrong here; it is simply what standing still costs when you refuse to stop advertising in a soft market.
🏦 The savings account cooling off
−$1.0m
The company sits on $137m and no debt, so it earns interest. Rates came down, so it earned less. Pleasant problem to have, but it still lands on the profit line.
So in one sentence: import taxes took a slice out of every order, price rises won back about a quarter of it, and everything else was a rounding error — which is why profit fell 12% while sales went up 1%.
Profit goes back up when: supplier prices stop climbing, or price rises catch up to what tariffs cost — the gap to close is the 1.3 cents per dollar lost since last summer. New customer numbers picking up would help, but on this company's own history that takes a couple of years to show up in profit.
