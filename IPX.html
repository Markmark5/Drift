💡Idiot's guide to the company — What Impax Asset Management actually does

What it is

A London fund manager that invests other people's money in companies it thinks will profit from the world moving to cleaner, more efficient goods and services — water, renewable power, energy-efficient kit, healthcare. It looked after £23.3bn at 30 June 2026, down from £37.2bn two years earlier.

Where it's based

Headquartered in London and listed on AIM, with a large US business (Impax NH) and a bond team in Copenhagen. Most of its revenue comes from clients outside the UK.

How it makes money

It charges an annual fee worth a fraction of a percent of the money it manages — last printed at about half a penny per pound a year. So income rises and falls with two things: whether clients put money in or take it out, and whether the shares it holds go up or down. Almost nine-tenths of the money sits in share funds; the rest is in bond funds (two teams bought in 2024–25) and private wind-and-solar funds, where fees are fixed on money investors promised.

Who controls the price. The fee rate is set fund by fund and rarely moves. What really decides revenue is clients: big pension schemes, wealth managers like St James's Place (which left in early 2025) and BNP Paribas, whose funds Impax runs and which provided a quarter of revenue in FY25 and owns about 14% of the company.

What's changed recently

Three years of clients pulling money as the market rewarded a handful of huge US tech stocks over Impax's style. It lost both St James's Place mandates (about £6bn), cut about 45 jobs in FY25 and is cutting about 30 more, halved the interim dividend, and in April 2026 said FY26 revenue would be about £109–113m, against £141.9m a year earlier. Investment performance improved sharply in early 2026. The Impax Environmental Markets trust ran an exit tender, and 77.8% of its shares left.

The bet, in one line: better investment returns since January bring clients back before the cost cuts run out of road.
