💡Idiot's guide to the company — What Hays plc actually does

What it is

Hays is a recruitment firm. Companies pay it to find them staff — either permanent hires (Hays takes a one-off fee per placement) or temporary workers and contractors (Hays takes a slice of every hour worked). The money Hays keeps after paying the temps is called net fees, and that's the number that matters — turnover includes the temps' wages, which just pass through.

Where it's based

London-listed, operating in 23 countries. Germany is the biggest single market, then UK & Ireland and Australia. About two-thirds of net fees now come from temp and contracting work, one-third from permanent placements.

How it makes money

Two engines. Temp & Contracting hums along steadily — workers on assignment keep generating fees week after week. Perm is the boom-bust engine: fees only land when someone is actually hired, so when bosses get nervous, hiring freezes and Perm fees vanish fast.

Who controls the price. The job market does. Hays can cut its own costs but cannot make clients hire. Wage inflation helps fees; hiring freezes crush them.

What's changed recently

Three years of shrinking fees forced deep surgery: a third of fee-earners gone, 74 offices closed in FY26 alone, six European businesses sold, and a new "Momentum" plan promising £50m more cost savings in FY27. The restructuring bills ran £89.6m in FY26, so the accounts show a statutory loss even as day-to-day profit turned up.

The bet, in one line: costs have been cut faster than fees are falling, so any hiring recovery drops straight into profit — if Perm behaves.

Notes — market backdrop & regulatory track


How to read the market table: the survey is direction-only (rises vs falls among ~400 UK agencies), covering white- and blue-collar; no magnitude read-across to Hays' fee percentages. The whole HAS record to Jun-26 sat against a UK perm market falling 45 consecutive months — UK&I's improvement from −20% (Q1 FY25) to −8% (Q4 FY26) happened entirely inside a shrinking market. The Jul→Sep-26 perm turn is the first UK market tailwind anywhere in the record, and it covers exactly the quarter Hays UK&I improved to −5% — direction agrees, first external corroboration of the recovery read. Caveat: UK&I is ~20% of group fees (Germany 32%, RoW 34%, ANZ 14% at Q1 FY27); this column says nothing about Germany, where the fragility is named.


The Oct-26 relapse is the live finding: it is the first external print inside the Q2 FY27 window — the quarter that contains December, Hays' habitual cut month (Jan-24 and Jan-25 both cut at the Q2 TU) — and both series turned red together on pre-Budget nerves. A Hays UK&I deceleration at the 14-Jan TU against this backdrop reads as market, not share; a deceleration after the column turns green again would be the worse signal. Score the market column before excusing or crediting any R1/R2 move.


Regulatory mechanism (why an agency cares about a hirer's duty): end hirers carry the guaranteed-hours offer, but a worker accepting one becomes directly engaged by the hirer — leaving the agency's book permanently, with no temp-to-perm fee attached. Hays' mitigations: its UK temps skew high-skill and full-assignment (above the consulted 8–20 hr threshold), and the compliance burden may push hirers toward MSP/Solutions management — a partial offset. The scoreable event remains language: Hays naming the Act as a headwind in the UK&I line scores 🔴🔴 under the lexicon; first mention of any kind is a disclosure event for §4.
