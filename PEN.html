Revenue, margin and profit all turned — but order book silence while Training Systems tripled is unresolved

💡Idiot's guide to the company — What Pennant International Group actually does

Pennant is a small Cheltenham-based technology company — about 100 people — that helps defence forces, airlines and rail operators run complex equipment without it breaking down. Think of it as the firm that writes the manual, builds the training simulator, and sells the software that tells a maintenance crew which part needs replacing and when.

Three ways it earns money

Auxilium software (recurring, high-margin): a suite of three applications — GenS (interactive electronic technical publications), Analyzer (equipment health monitoring), and R4i (data management) — now rebranded and sold together as Auxilium. Customers pay annual subscription fees. This is the bit the strategy is built around: high margins, sticky, grows without hiring more people.

Technical Services (repeatable, medium-margin): consultancy, support and maintenance work for users of the software and for third-party systems, mainly in the UK, Canada and Australia. Revenue is recurring in the sense that the same clients come back, but it's time-and-materials rather than contracted subscription.

Training Systems (project-based, lower-margin): designs and builds hardware and virtual training simulators for defence forces — cockpit trainers, vehicle simulators, maintenance-task trainers. Big contracts, lumpy revenue, recognised when milestones are hit. Was the dominant business for decades; now being actively shrunk relative to the software side.

Recent structural events

Restructured in 2024: Training Systems headcount cut, two Cheltenham properties sold for £3.1m, Canadian overarching contract expired and replaced with individual tenders. CFO Michael Brinson left Sep-24; replaced by interim and then permanent Darren Wiggins. Chair Phil Cotton stepped down after AGM 2024; replaced by Ian Dighé. Going-concern material uncertainty in auditor reports from FY22 through FY25 — resolved by property disposals, equity raise (£1.25m Sep-25) and a £0.3m shareholder loan from a director. Shareholder loan repaid Jul-26.

The investment argument: if ARR keeps compounding and the margin on software is genuinely 50%+, the business looks very different at £5m ARR than at £2.5m — the multiple of what it earns on each new subscription pound is the lever. As at Jul-26.

WHAT THE TU SAYS (verified against source)

H1 FY26: revenue c.£5.9m (+31% on H1 FY25 £4.5m) · ARR £2.6m · gross margin 53% · adjusted EBITDA +£0.5m (H1 FY25: −£1.0m loss) · adjusted LBT £0.3m (H1 FY25: £1.7m) · Auxilium investment £0.7m · net debt £0.9m. Guidance: "on track to achieve full year 2026 market expectations"; H2 expected stronger than H1. Interims due 1 Sep 2026.

Segmental split is the real story. Training Systems revenue tripled — £0.6m → £1.9m (+217%), driven by Engineered Training £0.4m → £1.8m (+350%). Software & Services was near-flat at £4.0m (+3%), with Systems Support Software +25% to £1.5m but Technical Services down 7% to £2.5m. R1 — Order book withheld. Scored ❌ 🔴. Judgement call, resolved against the company.

No three-year order book figure anywhere in the TU. The narrow cadence test would have made this a neutral —: at H1-TU grain the metric was printed in Aug-21, Jul-22 and Jul-23, then omitted in Jul-24, Jul-25 and now Jul-26, and a patchy habit is normally a —. I have scored it ❌ anyway, and the reasoning matters more than the cell.

The patchiness here is not structural drift in what a trading update contains — it is the same withholding behaviour repeating. Across five years the pattern is consistent and one-directional: the order book is published when it flatters and dropped when it does not. It was quantified in ten consecutive documents while it sat at £22–27m; it vanished for eight consecutive documents covering the entire period it fell from £25m to £15.9m; that fall was disclosed only in Mar-26, retrospectively, as a comparator alongside the recovery. Decisive point: the company itself re-established the cadence four months ago — it headlined £23.3m as "substantially strengthened" and volunteered that Training Systems alone had grown to £10.3m. A metric restored with that much emphasis is back in cadence; omitting it in the very next RNS is a withhold against a habit management had just re-set.

The innocent reading exists and is worth stating. Training Systems revenue tripled in H1, which consumes order book, so a lower position would be arithmetically expected even with healthy intake — and a bullet-point TU is an awkward place to explain that. But that is an argument for disclosing and explaining a fall, not for silence. The £1.0m initial order for the air-defence simulator is named; a single order is not a book position. This fires deterioration condition 3 (Drift). It does not flip the direction — all three hard-number ranks turned decisively — but it is the named caveat against an otherwise clean turn, and the 1 September interims must answer it.

R2 — ARR £2.6m: growth re-accelerated, and my earlier concern is partly answered.

Sequential: Dec-24 £1.9m → Jun-25 £2.3m (+£0.4m) → Dec-25 £2.4m (+£0.1m) → Jun-26 £2.6m (+£0.2m). The H2 FY25 stall did not continue. I flagged after the last audit that the OmegaPS→GenS migration pool was half-spent and the driver looked exhausted; the H1 FY26 step-up suggests new sources are contributing — the TU names the first Siemens-channel sale, the Canadian DND contract, and ConvertWise. That is a better-quality £0.2m than the migration-driven £0.4m was.

The guide is still the tension. Management guides ARR to exceed £3.0m by end-FY26. From £2.6m that needs +£0.4m in H2, double the H1 increment. Achievable if the new channels convert, but it is not the run-rate. This remains the single most informative number in the 1 September interims.

R3 — Revenue: the turn is real, but read the composition.

+31.3% year-on-year against −39.1% in H1 FY25 — a Δrate of +70pp, the largest single-period swing in the record. This resolves the contested H2 FY25 cell in favour of the optimistic reading: the decline was genuinely bottoming, not merely lapping a soft comp. Two caveats. First, £5.9m is a "c." estimate in an unaudited TU — the later audited figure governs, and PEN's own history shows its rounded halves drifting up to 0.1m from statutory. Second, H1 FY25 (£4.49m) was the weakest half in the record, so +31% off that base still leaves H1 FY26 below every half from FY21 to FY24. Recovery, not yet growth.

Against consensus: £5.9m is 45% of the £13.0m FY26 consensus, implying H2 of £7.1m — a 20% step up on H1. The company says H2 will be stronger, which is habitual for PEN (H2 > H1 in every year of the record), so this is consistent rather than heroic. But it is a real second-half weighting on a company whose H2 has missed before.

This is not concealment and I am not scoring it as a fire. The substitute is fully quantified, more favourable, and consistent with how the broker now models the company — the direction is unambiguously positive whichever line you use. It is a re-benchmarking, not a withholding. But it does break the comparability of Rank 4's series at exactly the moment the series turns, and anyone reading £0.5m against last year's EBITA numbers will overstate the improvement by about £0.2m.

LEDGER DECISION — Rank 4 stays on Adjusted EBITA. No migration to EBITDA.

The obvious move is to follow the company onto EBITDA: it is now their disclosure line, the consensus line, and the only basis on which H1 FY26 compares forward. I am not doing it, for one reason that is specific to how this company is built. PEN capitalises £0.7m of Auxilium development every half — £1.4m a year, unchanged year on year and explicitly disclosed in this TU. That spend does not hit the income statement when incurred; it lands later as amortisation. EBITDA excludes amortisation and depreciation both, so on this company it is precisely the line that hides the cost of the strategy it is being used to celebrate. Adopting it would mean rebasing the ledger onto management's more favourable presentation at the exact moment management changed it — which is the behaviour this framework exists to detect, not to imitate.

The cost of holding the line is small and temporary. The 1 September interims will print amortisation, depreciation and net interest, at which point H1 FY26 adjusted EBITA becomes derivable by the method already validated against FY24 (±£1k). So this is one ❌ carried for roughly seven weeks, not a permanent hole. Carrying it honestly also preserves the disclosure signal — migrating would erase the fact that the basis changed at all.

Standing watch item added: capitalised development of £0.7m per half against adjusted EBITDA of £0.5m means the company capitalised more than it earned on its own headline profit measure. That is not damning — it is what a software transition looks like — but it is the number that makes EBITDA unsafe here, and it should be checked at every print. If capitalisation keeps running above EBITDA while ARR growth stays around £0.2m a half, the strategy is consuming more cash than it is generating recurring revenue, whatever the profit line says.
