💡Idiot's guide to the company — What discoverIE Group plc actually does

What it is

discoverIE designs and makes bespoke electronic bits — sensors, connectors, magnetics, power supplies — that get buried inside other companies' industrial kit. It doesn't sell to you or me; it sells the fiddly custom components an equipment-maker can't easily buy off a shelf, which is why once its part is designed into a customer's product, it tends to stay there for that product's whole life.

Where it's based

UK-listed (FTSE 250), run from Guildford, but the real business is spread across mainland Europe, the UK, China, Sri Lanka, India and North America — around 4,600 staff in 21 countries.

How it makes money

Two halves. Magnetics & Controls and Sensing & Connectivity — both making custom components aimed at markets set to grow for years whatever the economy does: industrial automation, medical, renewable energy, transport electrification, security. Growth comes two ways: winning new "design-ins" that lock a part into a customer's next product (organic), and buying up small specialist component makers to bolt on (acquisitions — 30 deals in 15 years). The order book and design-win pipeline are the tell for where organic sales go next.

Who controls the price. The customer's engineers pick the part when they design their machine, so once a discoverIE component is in, swapping it means a redesign. That gives the company more say on price than a parts catalogue would — though the big equipment makers still haggle hard on volume deals.

What's changed recently

Three bolt-on buys in a year — Storm (Dec-25), Trival (Apr-26) and 3Gmetalworx ($67.5m, announced May-26, regulatory approval well progressed) — about £95m committed, the biggest such run in the Group's history. Borrowing stood at 1.6x a year's profit at Sep-26, guided to about 1.8x once 3G completes.

The bet, in one line: a serial compounder that buys niche component specialists, embeds them in customers' products for the long haul, and funds the next deal from reliable cash — worth owning while design wins and the order book keep climbing and the debt keeps falling.

🏭 Selling more, the hard way
+£0.5m op. profit
Sales grew 5% to £443m, but day-to-day profit only crept up 1% to £61.0m — because they deliberately spent ahead, kitting out factories in Asia and hiring sales and engineering muscle in the US and Europe. Jam tomorrow: the investment lands now, the payback comes later.
🔧 Building for a bigger tomorrow
held it to +1%
That same spending is why the profit slice of each pound of sales dipped a touch (13.8%, down 0.4 of a point). It's a choice, not a stumble — they're buying capacity now to hit a 17%-margin goal by 2030, part-funded by squeezing costs elsewhere.
💸 The bank takes a smaller cut
+3pts of PBT
Here's the lift: less money owed and falling interest rates meant a smaller bill for borrowed cash. That's what lifts whole-company profit up 4% when the shop-floor profit only rose 1% — the gap is pure interest saving. Net debt fell £14m to £80.5m over the year.
🪙 More left over per share
EPS +4% → 40.3p
After a lighter interest and tax bill, each share earned 40.3p, up from 38.7p — the tenth straight year of earnings growth (14% a year averaged over the decade). Enough to nudge the dividend up 4% to 13.0p and still cover it three times over.
So in one sentence: they sold more and paid the bank less, so profit rose 4% even though they poured money into future growth that flattened this year's margin. Profit accelerates from here when: the Asia and US investment starts earning its keep, net debt keeps falling faster than rates, and the three new acquisitions (bought for £95m) bed in at their higher margins.
