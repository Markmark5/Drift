💡Idiot's guide to the company — What Forterra plc actually does

What it is

Forterra makes the heavy stuff a house is built from: clay bricks, concrete blocks and the concrete floors that sit between storeys. It is one of three big brick makers left in Britain. It owns its clay — over 90 million tonnes of it, roughly fifty years of digging — with a quarry beside almost every factory, which is the whole reason nobody new can easily turn up and compete. Getting planning permission for a fresh quarry and kiln can take a decade.

Where it's based

Head office in Northampton. Eight brick factories across seven sites, two aircrete block plants in the Midlands and South, two aggregate block plants in the South East and East, and the Bison flooring operation. Everything it sells, it sells in the UK.

How it makes money

Bricks and blocks — about eight pounds in every ten. Mostly sold to the big housebuilders and to builders' merchants. Two kinds of brick matter: extruded (wire-cut, cheaper, made in bulk) and soft mud (prettier, dearer, popular in the South East). Forterra is 80% extruded against an industry that is 65%, and that has been working in its favour while buyers are watching costs. It also owns the London Brick name — the Fletton brick in a quarter of England's older houses — which people buy to match an extension to what's already there.

Bespoke products — about two pounds in every ten. The Bison precast flooring business: beam and block ground floors and hollowcore upper floors. These go in at foundation stage, so they move a few months ahead of the bricks.

Who controls the price. Nobody, really. Prices are set once a year with big customers who have plenty of choice, and 2023 to 2024 was three years of not getting increases to stick. Gas is the largest single cost and is bought years ahead, which is why an energy shock hurts Forterra later than it hurts everyone else — and keeps hurting after the news has moved on.

What's changed recently

Six years and £140m went into new capacity: a huge new brick factory at Desford, a rebuilt Wilnecote aimed at architects rather than housebuilders, and a plant at Accrington making brick slips — thin brick tiles for cladding tall buildings. That programme is now essentially finished, so the money going out has dropped sharply. Two loss-making side businesses were shut in late 2025. A £20m buyback started in 2026.

The bet, in one line: a business with fifteen per cent more capacity than it had last cycle, running at about sixty per cent of it, waiting for Britain to start building again.

§2.0 · Current-state read

🍺IDIOT'S GUIDE TO THE ADJUSTED EBITDA BRIDGE — why profit went down (£29.9m → £27.0m)
🧱 Kilns hotter than the order book
−£3.3m
Bricks and blocks earned £36.3m before head-office costs, against £39.6m. Brick held up best; aircrete blocks fell hardest, because the rivals who couldn't supply in 2023 are back in the market. Wet weather stole the spring, then a war stole the summer.
🏗️ Floors with nobody standing on them
−£1.4m
The flooring arm made £4.0m against £5.4m. Beams and hollowcore go in at foundation stage, so this is the first place a housebuilder slowing down shows up — sales fell 14.4% once you strip out the business they shut last year.
✂️ Head office on a diet
+£1.8m
Central costs charged down to the two divisions dropped from £15.1m to £13.3m. Managers and support staff were cut, saving around £2m a year. It cost £3.0m in redundancy money to get there, and that bill sits outside this profit figure.
So in one sentence: the factories lost £4.7m of profit because Britain stopped buying, and head office handed £1.8m of it back by shrinking — leaving £2.9m less than last year.
Profit goes back up when: builders start ordering again and the stock in the yards starts moving; the April surcharges and the June brick increase stick without customers walking; and the gas bought at pre-war prices doesn't run out before the fighting stops.
