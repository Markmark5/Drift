💡Idiot's guide to the company — What Warpaint London actually does

Warpaint is a British seller of cheap-and-cheerful make-up. Think the lipstick, mascara and nail varnish you grab near the till in Boots, Superdrug or Tesco — good enough to look the part, priced so nobody winces. It's run out of London and listed on AIM (ticker W7L).

Where the money comes from

Two engines. W7 is the flagship colour-cosmetics brand — the biggest single slice of sales, sold through UK shops and, increasingly, across Europe and a toe in the US. Technic is the gifting and Christmas-set business (the make-up advent calendars and stocking-fillers), plus some own-label work making product that carries a supermarket's own name. The company buys most of its stock from factories in Asia, paid for in US dollars — which is why exchange rates matter so much to it.

Who's in charge

Founder-led and founder-owned: CEO Sam Bazini and MD Eoin Macleod between them hold roughly 40% of the shares, so the people running it have most of their own net worth riding on it.

Recent shape-changing events

It went shopping. In February 2025 it bought Brand Architekts (Skin & Tan, Super Facialist, Dirty Works, Fish Soho) — a set of skincare and personal-care brands to sit alongside the make-up. In February 2026 it grabbed the Barry M brand cheaply out of administration. Both add revenue but also muddy the "how's the core business really doing?" picture.

The investment case in one line: a cash-generative, debt-free, founder-run value make-up maker rolling its brands into more shops worldwide — the question now is whether organic growth has genuinely stalled or just paused.

🍺IDIOT'S GUIDE TO THE PROFIT BRIDGE — why profit went down (£10.8m → £6.4m)
📦 More stuff off the shelves
+£1.4m
They shifted more lipstick and mascara than the same six months last year. This is the honest, boring engine of the business doing exactly what it's meant to — no tricks, just more product sold, more money in.
💰 Charging a bit more, keeping more of it
+£1.2m
On every pound of product sold, more of it stuck as profit than before — better sourcing from the factories and a small price rise meant the stuff they sold was simply worth more to them than it used to be.
🧾 The wage bill and the bigger American road-crew
−£2.7m
Running the shop got dearer. More salaries, higher business rates, marketing spend, and a beefed-up US sales team hired to chase bigger customers — all money spent now in the hope it pays off later, but a real bite out of profit today.
💱 A currency bet gone sour
−£5.4m
This is the villain. Warpaint buys stock in US dollars and places bets months ahead to lock in exchange rates. Those bets moved the wrong way and showed a £4.6m loss — but here's the catch: most of it (£2.7m) is only on paper, an IOU that hasn't actually left the bank account and could swing back. It flatters nothing and hurts the headline, but it isn't cash out the door yet.
🎉 The "we bought it cheap" windfall
+£3.9m
When they bought Brand Architekts, its bits and pieces were worth more on paper than the price paid — so the accountants hand you an instant one-off gain, like finding a tenner in a coat you bought at a jumble sale. Nice, but it happens once and never again.
🧻 The bill for doing the deal
−£1.3m
Lawyers, bankers and advisers don't buy a company for you out of kindness. One-off fees for getting the Brand Architekts deal over the line — gone as soon as it's done.
So in one sentence: the real shop — more sold, at fatter margins — actually grew, but a soured currency bet (mostly still just paper) and the one-off racket of buying a company dragged the printed number down, with a lucky accounting windfall from that same deal quietly filling part of the hole.

Profit goes back up when: the currency bets either come good or roll off the books, the one-off deal costs stop recurring (they're already behind them), and the steady growth in volume and margin — both still pointing up — gets to shine through without the noise sitting on top of it.
