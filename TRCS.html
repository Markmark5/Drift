💡Idiot's guide to the company — What Tracsis actually does

What it is
Tracsis sells software and kit to the people who run trains. If you have ever tapped a bank card at a station barrier, claimed money back for a late train, or seen a box bolted to the track counting how the rails are behaving, there is a decent chance a bit of Tracsis was involved.

Where it is
Head office in Leeds. Big teams in Ireland doing satellite and mapping work. A smaller operation in New York State selling to American railroads.

How the money comes in
Rail Technology & Services — roughly £38m a year. Train companies pay a licence every year to use the software that builds timetables and rosters. Network Rail pays for the safety system its whole supply chain now runs on. And every card tap and every refund claim earns a small fee, which adds up because there are millions of them. Customers here are train operators and Network Rail, so the buying is set by government funding cycles, not by the market.

Data, Analytics & Consultancy — roughly £44m a year. Counting traffic for councils, satellite mapping for Irish government departments, and until this month, marshalling the crowds at festivals and football. That last part has just been sold.

What just happened
Two deals in six days, either side of the year end. On 29 July they agreed to buy Mistral Data from FirstGroup for £48m — a rail software business where about 85 pence in every pound comes back automatically each year. On 3 August they sold the events crowd-marshalling business for £7.25m. They are swapping a people-heavy job for a software one, and borrowing to do it. The company has never had debt before.

The argument for owning it: a company with a genuine grip on software the railway cannot easily switch off, trading at about twelve times profits, deliberately trading size for quality — with the catch that its biggest customer is being taken apart and rebuilt by the government while it does so.

Turn methodology & restatement basis

Comparison basis per rank. Ranks 1, 4, 5 are flows on the half spine, compared year-on-year with the banded change in growth rate carrying the score. Ranks 2 and 3 are rate metrics, scored on the percentage-point move year-on-year directly.

Display basis. Cells show the year-on-year figure. The sequential comparison is computed and retained here, never displayed. Percentage-point values and rate-change labels never render in cells — the emoji alone carries the score.

Stated unit per rank. R1 £000 · R2 % · R3 % · R4 £000 · R5 £000. Identical on the section bar, the row label and the rendered values.

Zero-crossing guard. Fired on five Rank 1 cells (H1 FY22 through H1 FY24) where the base falls below £200k or the prior year's growth rate is itself void. Those cells are scored on absolute change against a band of ±£544k, being 10% of the series' largest absolute value, and marked ‡.

Precision floor. Rank 2's H1 FY22 and H2 FY22 cells rest on a recurring-revenue figure derived from a company-stated 16% growth rate rather than a printed level. Marked ◊. Tested at the rounding bounds: at 15.5% the share reads 19.84%, at 16.5% it reads 20.53% — a spread of 0.69pp, inside the ±3.0pp band. Neither cell carries a score in any case, both being first-in-series.

Restatement and basis. Rank 2 carries a ʳ at H1 FY24. The company changed its recurring-revenue definition that year, from "annual recurring and routinely repeating revenue" (which included annually repeating hardware framework revenue) to "recurring software licence revenue". Both are printed for FY24: £25.5m and £21.8m. This is a definitional change, not a restatement, and no arithmetic crosses it. The change coincided with the collapse in Remote Condition Monitoring hardware — the component the old definition included.

Cash basis break. FY22 and H1 FY23 cash figures include £2.2m of RailComm escrow; FY23 onward is cash only. Cash is a WATCH metric and does not enter the grid.

Later governs earlier. Every "c." estimate in a trading update is superseded by the audited figure. 🎪 Marshalling the crowds for less+£0.7m Same festivals, same football, fewer people in hi-vis standing about. They put prices up and stopped swallowing wage rises. This is the bit they've just sold.
