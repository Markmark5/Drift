💡Idiot's guide to the company — What Raspberry Pi Holdings actually does

What it is

Raspberry Pi designs tiny, cheap, surprisingly powerful computers — credit-card-sized boards from about $4 to $165 — plus its own microcontroller chips. Hobbyists made it famous; industrial customers building the boards into machines, kiosks and gadgets now make most of the money (about 70% of demand).

Where it's based

Cambridge, UK. Boards are manufactured in Wales at a Sony-run factory. Listed on the London Stock Exchange since June 2024.

How it makes money

Three streams: selling boards and modules directly to resellers and manufacturers; royalties from licensee Premier Farnell, which builds and sells some products under licence; and a fast-growing chip business selling its own RP-series microcontrollers to other device makers (8.4m chips in 2025, now more chips than boards). A small but growing software service, Connect, manages fleets of devices remotely.

Who controls the price. Raspberry Pi sets list prices and famously holds them low — but its biggest input, DRAM memory, is priced by a handful of giant chipmakers, and when they raise prices (as the AI boom has forced), Raspberry Pi must pass it on or absorb it.

What's changed recently

Memory prices roughly doubled from late 2025. The company stockpiled cheap memory ahead of the surge, raised product prices, and is now selling full-price boards built with bargain parts — a temporary profit windfall it says fades in H2 2026. Sony trimmed its stake in April 2026; a new CFO (Tim Powell) starts October 2026.

The bet, in one line: a beloved hobbyist brand quietly becoming an industrial-components company, where the chips may one day outgrow the boards.

📦 Four million little computersn/qThey shipped over 4 million boards, the most ever in a half — more boards, more profit, no cleverness required.
🧀 The cheap-memory lardern/qAll through 2025 they stuffed the warehouse with memory chips at old prices. Every board built now uses cheap chips but sells at today's higher price — the gap lands straight in profit.
💷 Price rises that stuckn/qThey put prices up to cover dearer memory — and customers kept buying anyway.
🎛️ Selling the posh onesn/qBuyers leaned toward the dearer, better-loaded models, so the average board earns more.
So in one sentence: record volumes met a warehouse full of bargain memory sold at full price, and profit nearly doubled. Profit falls when: the bargain larder runs bare (they say H2), fresh memory must be bought at today's prices, and any customer finally blinks at the new price tags.
