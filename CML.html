💡Idiot's guide to the company — What CML Microsystems Plc actually does

What it is

CML makes the specialist chips that let machines talk to each other over the airwaves — the mixed-signal, radio-frequency and microwave semiconductors inside two-way radios, satellite links, industrial sensors and defence kit. It doesn't make phones or laptops; it sells the small, high-reliability parts that go into other companies' professional communications gear.

Where it's based

Head office is Oval Park in Maldon, Essex, with design, test and sales arms in the USA (a Silicon Valley operation, MwT, bought in 2023) and Asia. It outsources the actual chip fabrication and does its own testing.

How it makes money

Two ways. Mostly it designs a chip then sells it by the reel, year after year — high-margin, repeatable product revenue. Increasingly it also gets paid up front to design custom silicon for a big customer (NRE — one-off design fees), which pays the bills now but at a thinner reward. A 12-year satnav deal signed in 2025 is currently in that design-fee phase. Who controls the price. Mostly CML: it is sole-source on most of its chips, so once a customer has designed a part in, switching is slow and expensive. Design fees are negotiated per contract and sit at a thinner reward.

What's changed recently

A multi-year reshaping: bought MwT (2023) to widen the microwave range, restructured UK research (2025, took a write-off), and sold surplus land at the Essex site for £7m to shore up cash. Now calls itself a pure-play communications semiconductor company chasing four markets: professional radio, network infrastructure, industrial IoT, and aerospace & defence.

The bet, in one line: a debt-free, dividend-paying niche chip firm that has just been through a bruising demand slump and a self-funded rebuild — the bet is that the order book is turning and profit follows the revenue back up.

Stable facts, as at 17 Nov 2026 (H1 FY27 results). A new CFO, Adrian Keane, joins 1 Feb 2027.

🍺IDIOT'S GUIDE TO THE OPERATING-PROFIT BRIDGE — why profit went up (−£0.98m → £0.19m)⭐ H1 R · 17 Nov 2026
📦 The reels are rolling again
+£3.15m rev
Chip sales jumped from £9.18m to £12.32m. Customers finished eating their 2024 stockpile and came back for more, in every region at once — Europe up 89%, the Americas 57%, Asia 11%. Each extra reel sold costs little to make, so most of that extra revenue drops straight into profit.
🛠️ Still moonlighting as an engineering firm
margin 67→64%
A bigger slice of the half was paid-by-the-hour design work for the satnav customer rather than chips off the shelf. Design fees keep the lights on but keep less of each pound — 64p instead of 67p. The satnav chips themselves don't ship until 2028.
🧪 Lab coats charged to this year's bill
−£0.84m costs
Running costs rose from £7.14m to £7.97m, and £0.78m of that is research work CML chose to write off as it went rather than park on the balance sheet for later. Strip that out and overheads grew 6% against revenue up 34%.
♻️ Tax credit, plus a bag of scrap gold
+£0.35m
Other income: £0.17m back from the taxman for research spending and £0.14m from reclaiming precious metal out of old parts. Small, welcome, not repeatable on demand.
So in one sentence: a third more chips out the door did nearly all the work, overpowering a thinner margin and a bigger research bill taken on the chin, and tipped the trading line from a £0.98m loss to a £0.19m profit. Profit falls when: the design-fee share keeps growing without the satnav chips shipping, the rebuilt £5.4m stockpile turns out to be ahead of demand again, or the "beyond two years" backlog is stretched out rather than pulled in.
