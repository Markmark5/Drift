💡Idiot's guide to the company — What Cerillion plc actually does

What it is

Cerillion sells the software a phone company runs its customers on — working out what each subscriber owes, sending the bill, taking the payment, and keeping the account records. Around 70 phone companies in about 45 countries use it.

Where it's based

Head office in London, with the build-and-support teams in India and Bulgaria, and salespeople in Europe, the USA, Asia and Australia. It was carved out of Logica in 1999 by Louis Hall, who still runs it, and joined the junior London market in 2016.

How it makes money

Three ways. It charges a fee for the right to use the software, usually over five years. It charges for the engineers who install it, which takes a year or two. Then it charges an annual fee to keep it running and patched.

Who controls the price. Cerillion does, largely — it sells one product rather than building something bespoke for each buyer, so it doesn't get haggled down on custom work. The catch is the other side of the deal: the phone company decides when it signs, and these are multi-year decisions made by committee. Cerillion controls what it charges and almost nothing about when.

The awkward part is how the fee gets counted. When a customer can finally switch the software on, Cerillion counts the whole five years of fees as sales that day, even though the money arrives over five years. So a single big win can make one six-month period look spectacular and leave the next one looking hollow.

What's changed recently

In January 2026 it signed Omantel, the main phone company in Oman, for about £42.5m over five years — its biggest deal ever. Almost all of the benefit is expected to land in the six months to September 2026. A new finance director started in 2026. In June 2025 the founder sold a tenth of the company for £45.6m, his first sale since 2017, cutting his stake from 30% to 20%; he agreed not to sell again for a year.

The bet, in one line: that one very large contract lands on time, in a business where nothing else in the year is big enough to cover it if it doesn't.

Stable facts, as at 1 June 2026.

Turn methodology & restatement basis


Comparison basis per rank. R1 year-on-year at FY grain. R2 sequential in cells, year-on-year on the aggregate row only. R3 and R5 year-on-year, scored on the banded change in that growth rate between consecutive like periods. R4 the percentage-point move year-on-year, taken directly.


Display basis. Half-grain flow cells display the year-on-year figure; the sequential comparison is computed and retained here, never displayed. No percentage-point values render in cells.


Units. R1 £'000 · R2 £m · R3 £m · R4 % · R5 £'000. FY21 and FY22 statements print in whole pounds; converted once, at the source table, to £'000.


Zero-crossing guard, fired on Rank 1. FY23's +691.5% is a percentage off a £765k base and exceeds the +200% tripwire. Rank 1 therefore scores the absolute change against a ±£606k band — the spec default of 10% of the series' largest value, 6,055 — marked ‡, and no percentages render. Every move in the row clears it by at least £110k.


Rank 2 basis break (ʳ). Through FY22 the company said its contracted book would convert "within the next 12 to 18 months". From FY23 the wording is "within 12 months". The 75%→45% step is a horizon change, not deterioration, and the two sides are never differenced. H2'23 therefore carries a value and no score, and the FY23 aggregate is unscored.


Two contracted bases at H1'25. That document applies c.45% to two balances — £40.6m at 31 March and £46.9m at 30 April. The 31 March position governs, because a 30 April reading is neither a full sub-period nor a fiscal year and never occupies a column. Using it instead would give 21.1 and turn the H1'25 cell from a 🟢 into a much larger one.


Precision floor, Rank 2. Every conversion rate is printed to the whole percent, and from FY23 with a "c." prefix; the H1 balances are printed to £0.1m. Carried through the subtraction, H1'22's −6.1% spans −7.6% to −4.8% and therefore straddles the ±5.0% band: the cell is marked ◊ and scored amber. Every other cell's range sits wholly one side of the band, though H1'25 clears it by 0.3pp and H1'23 by 0.9pp.


Availability test correction. Every full-year document carries an IFRS 15 note giving the contracted balance exactly, in pounds. Rank 2's H2 cells were rebuilt on those figures rather than the highlights rounding, which moved H2'21 from 26.2 to 26.1, H2'24 from 17.0 to 16.9 and H2'25 from 15.6 to 15.7. The interim statements carry no such note, verified across all five, so H1 cells stand on the highlights footnote.


Derivation precision. H2† for revenue and EBITDA is derived at source precision — whole pounds before FY23 — and rounded afterwards. Deriving from rounded millions first would have put H2'21 revenue out by £1k.


Rank 1 basis choice. Scored on the own-licence column, which runs the whole record on one basis; the column carries the whole of the initial-licence line in FY21, FY22 and FY23, so no year's figure is split by the later change. The column definitions themselves are printed only in the FY25 document and were read there. Third-party licence revenue was folded into the same line from FY24 and adds £355k in FY24 and £329k in FY25; including it would not change a single direction.


Rank 3 provenance. New orders appear in no statement or note at this company — narrative and highlights only, availability-tested across all ten results documents. A footnote excluding support and maintenance appears at the Apr-26 update and again at H1'26, and nowhere earlier in the record. The comparator it carries, £19.6m, matches H1'25's own print to the decimal, so the basis is unchanged and the footnote makes explicit what was already true. Its first appearance is a Drift note, not a basis break.


No score before a rate of change exists. Ranks 3 and 5 are scored on the change in the year-on-year figure between consecutive like periods. That needs two year-on-year readings, so the first scoreable cell is H1'23 — H1'22 and H2'22 carry a year-on-year percentage but no score, and render — rather than flat. The record starts at H1'21, which is why.


Rank 3 precision. H2† is derived from two figures each printed to £0.1m. Carried through, every Δrate range sits wholly one side of the ±2.0pp band — the narrowest, H1'24, spans −8.4 to −8.3pp. No cell reaches the precision floor.


Rendering rules. Ranks 2 to 5 render under the default half-year spine. Rank 1 renders under reduced grain, operator-confirmed, and carries no aggregate row because its value is the full-year total.

The four checks — workings behind the read


1 · Sequential (H1'26 vs H2'25†). Revenue −26.3%, adjusted EBITDA −52.6%, adjusted PBT −55.9%, margin −19.2pp. Context only, not a score: Cerillion's second half is structurally the larger one, so a first half always reads badly against it.


2 · Same type versus last year (H1'26 results vs H1'25 results). Revenue −13.9%, adjusted EBITDA −37.8%, adjusted PBT −40.9%. Not a base effect — the base was already down. Two-year stack against H1'24: revenue −20.0%, adjusted PBT −47.7%.


3 · Derivable sub-periods. Quarters are not derivable; the company prints none, and the Omantel win is dated "Q2" qualitatively with no figures. What is derivable is the back half the guide requires: revenue £34.9m (+42.7% year-on-year, against H2'25's +15.1%), adjusted EBITDA £17.8m (+35.8%), adjusted PBT £17.4m (+39.5%). That puts 66.0% of the year in the second half; the most back-loaded year in the record was FY25 at 53.9%. Required H2 margin is 51.0%, below H2'25's 53.6% — so the stretch is volume, not profitability.


4 · Guidance versus prior guide. April's "well-positioned to meet market expectations" became June's "well-positioned to deliver consensus market expectations". Unchanged. G is flat — no cut, no raise. Sized against consensus, the held guide implies full-year revenue +16.6% and adjusted PBT +5.2%.


Bounding what the book can cover. The twelve-month contracted bucket is £28.3m and excludes support and maintenance; support and maintenance runs at roughly £4.75m a half, derived from the £9.5m annualised figure. Even if the entire bucket landed inside these six months, that is £33.0m against a £34.9m requirement. The guide needs the licence lump and new orders won and delivered in the same half.





Notes — §2.0


The second-half explanation has been used twice running, at H1'25 and H1'26, which under the decomposition rule exhausts it as a discount — but it was honoured the first time, when H2'25 delivered +15.1% revenue and a record 53.6% margin. The difference now is that the ask is roughly three times larger.


The company's revenue policy is the mechanism behind the whole year: term licence revenue is counted in full when the customer gains the right to use the software, not spread across the contract. One five-year contract therefore books its licence value inside one six-month window, which is why the record shows a sawtooth — £3.8m, £0.8m, £6.1m, £2.8m, £2.1m — rather than a trend.


Cash generated from operations fell to £1.7m from £7.0m while money owed by customers rose £2.3m: the profit that was earned in the half largely has not arrived as cash.


Sequence worth holding, without inferring motive from it: the first revenue decline in the listed record was disclosed on 19 May 2025; the chief executive sold 10.3% of the company at 1500p on 12 June 2025, raising £45.6m; his twelve-month lock-in ran out around 16 June 2026, a fortnight after this announcement.


Two silences, and only one of them carries information. No announcement of any kind since 8 June 2026 is normal: no qualifying document has ever been issued between June and the October update in this record. No contract win since 8 January 2026 is the one to watch, because both the April update and these results rest the full-year guide partly on anticipated new orders from existing customers. Seven months is the second-longest such gap in the record — but £17.3m of last year's second-half orders were announced twenty-two days before the year end.


Six of the eight contract-win announcements in the record state that the win supports or underpins market forecasts. That anchor only ever appears alongside good news, which is why those documents inform this note and never a scored row.

§4 workings — notes



Notes — §4


Every cell was verified against its source document by pattern search across all twenty qualifying announcements, not inferred from document type. Twenty manifest rows, twenty Drift columns.


Cadence, established before any absence was recorded. Initial licence fees appear only in the annual revenue note — never at a half-year, in any year — so every non-annual cell is outside cadence, not withheld. Adjusted EBITDA appears in half-year updates and both results documents but never in a year-end update, which carries pre-tax profit instead. Recurring revenue appears only in results documents. Pre-tax profit is absent from every half-year trading update in the record.


Disclosure expansions, with start dates. Revenue was qualitative in the year-end update until Oct-23, quantified from then. Pre-tax profit likewise gained a numeric consensus anchor from Oct-23. New orders entered the year-end update at Oct-25 and the half-year update at Apr-26. The order book entered the half-year update at Apr-26.


The recurring-revenue break. Annualised through FY23; switched to an in-period measure at FY24 with the comparator restated; the in-period measure carried at H1'25 in place of the annualised one; the annualised measure restored at H1'26 on a widened definition adding term licence and third-party hosting revenue. Three presentations in three years. This is why the metric is a Drift row and not a ranked one.


New orders in the year-end update is a patchy habit, not a cadence. Quantified once, at Oct-25; described without a figure at Oct-21 and Oct-24; absent altogether at Oct-22 and Oct-23. Those two absences stay outside cadence rather than becoming withholds, and nothing fires on them.


Pre-tax profit at a year-end update is a position against someone else's number. From Oct-22 every year-end update states the company's profit as ahead of a named consensus figure rather than giving its own. All four are recorded the same way, against the anchor, so the beat ladder reads off one row.


Order-book characterisation. The book is quantified in every results document and described qualitatively in every year-end update — a consistent habit across the whole record, not a withhold. Qualitative commentary continuing through a window does not constitute going quiet.
