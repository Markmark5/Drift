Idiot's guide to the company — What Solid State plc actually does

Who they are

Solid State makes and sources the tough electronic parts that go inside other people's kit — the boxes and boards built to survive rain, vibration, heat, or the middle of the ocean, not just sit on a desk. Founded in 1971, listed on AIM since 1996, based in Redditch in the English Midlands, with about 475 people across seven UK factories and one in the US.

How it makes money — three shops under one roof

Systems — builds complete computer and communications boxes for defence and security customers, the kind that go in a vehicle or a comms station. Biggest and lumpiest of the three: one big government programme can move the whole division's number for a year. Power — designs and makes batteries and power supplies for industrial and defence kit; steadier, contract-driven work. Components — buys parts from other manufacturers and sells them on with technical advice attached; the smallest-margin but steadiest of the three.

Who's in charge

John Macmichael was appointed Chief Executive on 26 March 2026, having stepped in as interim CEO the previous November after the sudden loss of long-serving CEO Gary Marsh. Macmichael spent 20 years running the Components division before taking the top job — an internal promotion, not an outside hire, so the strategy carries on rather than resetting. Jon Baxter has stepped up to lead Components in his place, and each of the three divisions now has its own named managing director reporting straight to the board.

The trading brands

Steatite, Solsta, Custom Power, Pacer, Active Silicon, Gateway, Durakool, Ndura and Q-Par — nine brand names, one group, built up partly by buying small specialist firms over the years (three acquisitions in the last five).

The argument, in short: three unglamorous but hard-to-copy electronics businesses riding the same wave — more defence spending and more AI hardware needing tougher power and comms parts.

🍺 IDIOT'S GUIDE TO THE ADJ PBT BRIDGE — why profit went up (£5.0m → £8.6m)
🏭 Sold way more, same factory
+£3.6m
Sales shot up £29m. The building, the machines, the lights — all already paid for — so most of each extra sale just lands as profit. This one line IS the story.
💰 The bonus jar is back
−£3.3m
Good year, so the staff bonus pot fills up again — £3.3m of it, versus nothing last year. It's tucked inside the costs above, not on top. Fair enough: they earned it.
🧾 Smaller bill from the bank
+£0.3m
Less money owed and cheaper rates, so the interest bill dropped from £0.9m to £0.6m. Not much — but hey, it's free money.
So in one sentence: they sold a truckload more without buying a bigger factory, and that alone did it — the bonus that came back is just the price of a good year, and the bank taking a smaller cut is a nice little extra. Profit goes back up when: sales stay north of ~£150m so the full factory keeps paying off, and the bonus jar doesn't fill faster than the tills do.
