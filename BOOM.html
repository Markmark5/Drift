💡 Idiot's guide to the company — What Audioboom Group plc actually does

The one-liner

Audioboom sells the advertising slots inside other people's podcasts, and keeps a cut. It's a London-listed company (AIM: BOOM) run out of London and New York, with about 92% of its money coming from the United States.

How the money actually arrives

Premium (about half the money). A famous podcast host reads an ad out loud in their own voice. Audioboom sells that slot to the brand, collects the cash, and hands most of it back to the podcaster. The podcaster owns the show; Audioboom owns the sales relationship.

Showcase (about four in ten dollars, and growing fastest). Software slots ads into episodes automatically, so the same episode can carry different ads for different listeners. Nobody has to record anything. Audioboom keeps more of each dollar here, which is why the company talks about it constantly.

Sonic (roughly one dollar in ten). A small team that works directly for a handful of big brands planning podcast campaigns. It has been shrinking — four brands used it last year, down from five.

The catch worth knowing

To sign the biggest podcasts, Audioboom often promises the creator a minimum payment whether or not the ads sell. When advertising was weak in 2023 those promises turned into losses — two of them were bad enough to be written off separately, and both have now expired. The same mechanism reappeared, smaller, in the first half of 2026.

Recent structural events

Bought Adelicious (UK podcast network) in July 2025, adding roughly 20 million monthly listens. Ran a strategic review from October 2025 after press speculation about a takeover; the review concluded in June 2026 with the company staying independent. A $10m credit line was agreed in principle in July 2026.

Scale, as at July 2026

183 million downloads and video views a month, 58 million listeners, fifth-largest podcast publisher in the US. Revenue $80.4m in 2025; $45.7m in the first half of 2026 alone.

The argument in one line: a sales business whose costs barely move, so every extra advertising dollar drops mostly to profit — as long as the promises made to creators stay smaller than the money the ads bring in.

🍺 IDIOT'S GUIDE TO THE ADJUSTED EBITDA BRIDGE — why profit went up (US$1.8m → US$3.2m)
🎧 Eighty-three million extra listens showed up+$2.5mThe network got much bigger and the ad slots on it sold. After paying the podcasters their share, what Audioboom kept rose from $7.4m to $9.9m.
🧾 The wage bill declined to stay still−$1.1mStaff and running costs rose 18% while sales rose 30%. Costs grew slower than income — but they still grew, and they ate a third of the gain.
🤝 A few creators cashed their promisesn/qTo sign big shows Audioboom promises a minimum payment whether ads sell or not. Some of those promises were called in, quietly shaving the improvement. The company names it but won't size it.
So in one sentence: they sold a great deal more advertising, the wage bill grew slower than the sales did, and profit nearly doubled — with a small bite taken out by payments promised to podcasters.

Profit falls when: listens stop climbing and the ad slots go unsold; or the promises made to creators get called in faster than the ads pay for them; or the new signings arrive with richer guarantees than the last lot, so each extra dollar of sales brings less home.
