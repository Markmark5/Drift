💡Idiot's guide to the company — What hVIVO plc actually does

The business

hVIVO deliberately infects healthy paid volunteers with a virus, in a sealed London quarantine unit, to find out whether a client's vaccine or antiviral actually works. It is the largest facility of its kind anywhere. Instead of recruiting thousands of people and waiting for some to catch flu naturally, you infect forty volunteers on a Tuesday and read the answer in a fortnight.

Where it is

Head office and the quarantine unit are in Canary Wharf, London. Labs and clinical sites across the UK and Germany, the German ones bought in 2025.

How the money arrives

Four lines, all sold to drug companies:

  • Challenge trials — the infection business. Biggest contracts, lumpiest timing, client pays a non-refundable deposit up front and the rest as the trial runs.
  • Clinical trials — ordinary early-stage drug testing at its own sites, no deliberate infection.
  • Consulting — advising clients how to design and file a trial. Small tickets, gets hVIVO in the door early.
  • Laboratories — testing and long-term storage of samples. Small, repeats, and the storage contracts run for years.

Who controls the timing: the client, almost entirely. They decide when a trial starts, and until 2026 they could walk away from early-stage agreements cheaply. That is what went wrong in 2025.

What changed recently

Bought two German clinics and a sample-storage company in early 2025 for about £14m. Then in 2025 several clients cancelled or postponed challenge trials, revenue fell from £62.7m to £46.8m, the dividend stopped, and cash went from £44.2m to £14.3m. From 2026 the company only counts fully-signed trial contracts in its order book, having previously counted earlier-stage agreements too. In August 2026 it bought a third German clinic — CRS Berlin, dermatology and women's health — for almost nothing up front, paying instead 18% of that clinic's revenue for three years.

The argument for owning it: a genuinely hard-to-copy asset, no debt, and an order book that just doubled — against a client base that has proved it can walk away, and a second half that has to deliver twice what the first half did.

📘 Why £65m today is worth more than £71m in 2024 — what changed in how contracts are counted

Two stages of commercial commitment. When a pharma company decides to run a human challenge trial, the relationship with hVIVO passes through two contractual stages before a trial commences. The first is a Start-Up Agreement (SUA), which covers early preparatory work — challenge agent supply, protocol development, regulatory submission prep, site set-up. Real money, real obligations, but only for the front-end work. The main trial — quarantine, inoculation, dosing, data collection — is then contracted separately under a full Clinical Trial Agreement (CTA), which carries the bulk of the revenue.

Why cancellation risk differs. If a pharma client pulls out at SUA stage — before the CTA is signed — they pay for work done to date under the SUA but have no obligation for the main trial revenue. The client's sunk cost is low and their optionality is high. At CTA stage the calculation reverses: the client has committed to the full programme, typically paid a non-refundable quarantine booking fee, and faces a materially higher financial penalty to cancel. CTA-stage cancellation risk is meaningfully lower, though not zero — FY25 demonstrated that even CTAs can be terminated.

What hVIVO was reporting up to end-2025. The "weighted contracted orderbook" figures reported through Dec-24 (e.g. £80m Dec-23, £71m Jun-24, £67m Dec-24) included both SUA-stage and CTA-stage work. The company applied a weighting to reflect differing confidence levels, but the methodology wasn't disclosed in enough detail for an outsider to strip out the SUA component. In the FY25 results (Apr-26), hVIVO changed the definition: "Going forwards, only full Clinical Trial Agreements (CTAs) will be announced and included in our orderbook, instead of Start-Up Agreements (SUAs), where there remains a higher risk of trial cancellations." They simultaneously restated Dec-24 from £67m to £43.5m on the new CTA-only basis — a £23.5m reduction, roughly a third of the old reported figure.

Connection to the FY25 guide blowup. The May-25 cancellations that triggered the £73m → £47m guide cut were described as "a significant HCT contract cancellation alongside a postponement and a smaller study cancellation." The record strongly implies these were at least partly SUA-stage or early-CTA work where clients retained cheap optionality to exit. That is the mechanism: SUA-stage entries look like contracted revenue but carry embedded client optionality that only shows up when macro conditions deteriorate.

What this means for the Jun-26 £65m. The Jun-26 figure is entirely CTA-only. Every contract in that number has crossed the threshold to a full Clinical Trial Agreement, with the client committed to the main programme and the non-refundable fee typically paid. Comparing it to the old-methodology Jun-24 £71m as if they're the same thing is like comparing a signed, deposit-paid order to a book that includes letters of intent and provisional bookings. The £65m is a better quality indicator of real forward revenue than the £71m was, even though it's nominally smaller and the business was presumably in better underlying health in mid-2024.

Residual watch. Only two data points exist on the CTA-only basis (Dec-25: £30m; Jun-26: £65m). The key test over the next two to three reporting periods is whether the new definition holds clean — i.e. whether some new form of early-stage commitment gradually re-enters how management communicates pipeline. Proposal volumes up 45% YoY represent the pre-SUA funnel; the conversion rate from proposals → SUAs → CTAs is the number to watch.

⚠️ STANDING LIMITATION & OPERATOR REMINDER — CONTRACT CONCENTRATION

What the record actually shows. Nothing in the disclosed leading metrics caught the May-25 guide cut in advance. At Dec-24 the orderbook was £67m, coverage was "70% of 2025 contracted ex-ILiAD", and the pipeline was described as a record. The earliest visible signal in the entire record was the derived H2 FY24 revenue decline of −5.6% — hidden inside a headline record year (FY24 +11.9%) and only surfaced by FY-minus-H1 derivation.

Why. For a milestone-billed HCT model the cliff risk lives in contract concentration — a single large cancellation moves the guide by a third. hVIVO has never disclosed orderbook composition at a grain the framework can score: no top-client %, no largest-contract value, no HCT-vs-other split within the orderbook. The tracker is therefore structurally blind to the exact risk that caused the drawdown. Proposals, coverage % and even the orderbook level are all aggregates that a concentrated book can carry right up to the cancellation.

Standing instruction — fires at every incremental read. On each new RNS pasted into this project, before any other output, check for and flag on sight: (1) any concentration disclosure — top-client %, largest-contract value, orderbook composition, HCT-vs-other split, or a named single contract material to guidance; (2) any single-contract cancellation, postponement or deferral, however framed; (3) any derived sub-period (H2 = FY − H1) that falls while the aggregate rises — the FY24 pattern; (4) the proposals-vs-orderbook wedge re-opening; (5) contracted coverage % (now Rank 5) re-quantified — a ranked green shoot, flag immediately — or proposals (watchlist) disclosure going qualitative/silent (red); (6) the Jan-27 orderbook test. The Jan-26 policy restricted orderbook disclosure to FY and H1 results, then Jul-26 overrode it the moment the number doubled. Disclosure here is demonstrably asymmetric, so silence is evidence: an orderbook figure in the Jan-27 trading update reads as strength and is expected if the book is still building; its absence reads as weakness and defers the reading to 15 Apr 27. Either way, net CRS Berlin's acquired ~€10m (27 Aug 26) out of the Dec-26 book before any book-to-bill or required-vs-won reading — an unsplit print is a disclosure finding. Flag whichever occurs, and treat a ⊘ against a build-phase expectation as a fired condition in its own right. The same logic applies to coverage % — at 90% contracted it is a boast, and it should reappear when it reads well; (7) contract-award RNSs. Noise for row purposes, but log each on arrival in the contract-award drawer — client, nature, phasing, and whether a value is given. Since Apr-26 hVIVO has stopped disclosing award values, so a running bookings total is not available; the return of a headline value would itself be a green shoot and must be flagged, as would any further award arriving as RNS Non-Regulatory. Each of these must be raised explicitly in the incremental read, whether or not it changes the direction call.

Turn methodology & restatement basis
Turn methodology: Orderbook and cash (snapshot metrics) compare position-to-position at the same disclosure grain — Jun-end vs Jun-end, Dec-end vs Dec-end; flow metrics compared like-period-to-like-period only (H1 vs H1, FY vs FY; never H1 vs FY or H1 vs H2). EBITDA margin is a period rate — H1 vs H1, FY vs FY, with the ±2.0pp band applied to the Δ(rate) between seasonally-matched periods. H2 EBITDA and revenue derived as FY minus H1 and marked †; cross-verified via H1+H2=FY identity. A derived figure in an aggregate period that is positive while a derived sub-period is negative flags the aggregate as backward-loaded (FY25 EBITDA: 3.0% aggregate, H2 = −7.1%). Turn arrows from script only; no arrows by eye. Net book-to-bill renders as a derived line under Rank 1, not a ranked metric: it is unscored, carries no turn arrow, and never counts toward the 2-of-3.
