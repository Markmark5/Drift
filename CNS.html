💡 Idiot's guide to the company — What Corero Network Security actually does

What it is

Corero sells kit and software that keeps websites and networks online when someone tries to flood them with junk traffic. That attack is called a denial-of-service attack: thousands of machines hammer a target at once until real customers can't get through. Corero's box sits at the edge of a network, spots the junk in real time and throws it away while letting genuine traffic past. Its customers are the people who run the pipes and the data centres — telecoms carriers, hosting firms, cloud providers — plus some banks, hospitals and government bodies.

Where it is

Listed in London on AIM as CNS, and quoted in the United States on OTCQX as DDOSF. Head office in London, engineering and operations in Marlborough, Massachusetts and in Edinburgh. Reports in US dollars. Financial year ends 31 December.

How the money arrives

Three streams, and which one a customer picks decides when Corero gets paid. Upfront licences and appliances: the customer buys the software and hardware outright, pays most of it at signing, and Corero books nearly all the sale straight away. Subscriptions, including the as-a-service product: the customer rents instead of buying, usually on a three-year deal, pays monthly or quarterly, and Corero books the sale in slices across the whole term. Support and maintenance: an annual fee attached to whatever the customer already has.

The customer chooses, not Corero. That matters more than anything else on this page. The same signed contract can look like a big year today or five small ones spread out, depending purely on which way the customer wants to pay. Roughly nine tenths of every sales dollar survives the cost of delivery, so the profit swings almost entirely with which stream is growing.

A lot of selling now goes through partners rather than Corero's own sales team — Akamai, Juniper, Hewlett Packard Enterprise, and regional resellers in Brazil and the Middle East. Partners widen the reach and slow the feedback: Corero learns later what its own pipeline is doing.

What changed recently

During 2025 customers swung hard toward renting. Sales that would once have landed in one lump got spread over three years instead. Revenue and profit fell even though the order book grew, cash got tighter because the money now arrives in instalments, and Corero arranged a bank overdraft it has not yet used. The contracted subscription base finished 2025 far above what the accounts were recognising, which is why 2026 revenue has jumped so hard.

The argument for owning it: a small company with genuinely good technology in a market that keeps growing, now sitting on three years of pre-sold revenue — provided it keeps adding new pre-sold revenue at the same rate, which in the first half of 2026 it did not.
